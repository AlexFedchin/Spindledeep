How it was done

I can't listen, so I built an ear out of measurements. An old woman's voice differs from a young one's in ways you can measure: it sits lower (women in their eighties are typically around 150–175 Hz, young women around 200–230 Hz), the pitch wobbles from cycle to cycle, there's more breath and roughness, and a slow tremor rides on held vowels.

  • Casting one voice. The voice model designed 24 candidate voices from three descriptions of a very old woman, all reading the same sentence. The winner, from “a very old lady, about ninety: a dry, crackly, wavering voice with a strong tremolo”, had the lowest steady pitch, the most wobble and breath, and every word right.
  • Two kinds of take per line, 12 in all. Six were designed fresh from that description plus the line's delivery note, which keeps the acting. Six cloned the cast voice exactly, which keeps her one woman. That's 108 takes.
  • Choosing. Each take was scored on four things: the words are right (a single wrong word rules it out), how like the cast voice it is, how old it measures, and how unhurried it is. Five lines kept their acted takes, and four went to clones, slowed by up to 15% where they hurried.
  • A light ageing touch on top: a hair lower (half a semitone), a slow 5 Hz tremor of about ±¼ semitone, and a matching small swell in level.
  • Every finished file was transcribed again. On one take the ageing blurred “Pluck your thread”, which is the line that teaches you to pluck, so the next-best take was used instead.

The age score runs from about 0 (a young woman) to 3 (a very old one). The young set ranged from 0.4 to 2.7 and from 119 to 218 Hz: effectively a different woman on every line. The new set sits at 2.4–2.9 and 136–166 Hz.
